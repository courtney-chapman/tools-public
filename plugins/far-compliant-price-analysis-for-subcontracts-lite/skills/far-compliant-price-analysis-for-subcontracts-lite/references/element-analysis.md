# Cost element and modification analysis

## Check the numbers that matter

Analyze the **overall proposed price** first. A supported comparison can
establish its reasonableness without a separate fair-and-reasonable finding
for every disclosed cost element. Examine elements when certified-data rules,
the applicable clause or policy, noncompetitive pricing, negotiation, an
unexplained variance, or the facts require more support. State whether the
work is price analysis, supplemental cost analysis of other data, or a
separate certified-data cost analysis. Show calculations that help the buyer
understand the proposed amount:

- Reconcile line items, quantities, rates, periods, and fee to the stated
  subcontract total. Display a discrepancy separately; do not silently
  replace the supplier's proposed amount.
- Analyze the latest submitted or negotiated price for the proposed award.
  If an initial quote, revised quote, or negotiated amount is supplied,
  distinguish each version, calculate the change, and state the supported
  reason. Do not treat a list-price difference as negotiated savings or
  double-count a discount in the final price.
  Identify the final quote or proposal revision and its date, the comparison
  evidence and dates, and the analysis date when known. Make clear which
  version the conclusion supports. If the analysis occurred after award or
  execution, flag the timing and do not present it as a timely pre-award
  analysis.
- When separately priced phases, base and option periods, or line items are
  available, check for material front-loading, back-loading, or unusually high
  or low prices relative to work and payment timing. Explain the concrete
  price or performance risk and obtain support where needed; a pricing pattern
  alone does not prove the offer unacceptable. Do not infer imbalance from a
  total price without a breakdown.
- For labor, compare disclosed rates and, if supplied, hours, labor categories,
  period values, and rate changes. Do not invent hours or treat their absence
  as an automatic gap.
- For cost-plus-fixed-fee pricing, show estimated cost plus fixed fee and the
  fee percentage using the disclosed cost base. For time-and-materials or
  labor-hour pricing, assess stated rates and ceilings. For fixed-price
  pricing, assess the proposed total, units, periods, or deliverables that
  support it.
- Calculate escalation only when comparable rates across periods exist.
  Compare it with a relevant supplied benchmark or supported market evidence
  before calling it reasonable or excessive.
- Normalize different periods by month, year, hour, seat, or unit only when
  the scope and period data support that comparison. State the normalization
  basis.
- Evaluate indirect rates and fee separately only when their values and bases
  are disclosed. A fully burdened rate can be compared as a total rate; it
  cannot reveal an undisclosed indirect rate or fee.
  For a material disclosed indirect-rate finding, identify the allocation
  pool and base, check that the proposed rate applies to the stated base, and
  consider the period and any supported historical or forward-rate comparison.
  A high or low percentage alone does not establish reasonableness; avoid
  double-counting costs assigned to another pool or direct element.
- For materials, lower-tier subcontracts, travel, and other direct costs,
  assess amounts needing separate review against available quotes, estimates,
  quantities, history, or other support. A lower-tier amount may need its own
  supporting cost or price analysis. State travel reimbursement terms or a
  technical review only when the record establishes them.
- Treat software subscriptions, licenses, or other items that are the primary
  subcontract deliverable as the subject of the overall price analysis. Do
  not put them under Other Direct Costs unless the proposal actually prices
  them as ancillary costs.

### Materials and other direct costs

When materials or ODCs are significant or separately priced, analyze the
actual proposed amount rather than merely listing the category. Scale the
detail to value, risk, contract type, and available evidence:

- **Reconcile the price.** For each material or ODC line with data, check
  quantity × unit price, term × recurring rate, or trips × units against its
  extension; then reconcile line totals, disclosed freight, taxes, discounts,
  and separately applied burden or fee to the proposed subtotal and overall
  subcontract value. Show discrepancies without silently changing the quote.
  Avoid counting an item or markup twice across labor, indirects, materials,
  and ODCs.
- **Compare the same purchase.** For materials, record the part/model/SKU or
  supported equivalent, condition or configuration, quantity and pricing
  tier, unit of measure, delivery location and timing, freight, warranty or
  support, and quote validity when material. Compare delivered prices on a
  common basis. For substitutes or similar items, state the differences and
  use actual technical confirmation when equivalence affects the finding.
- **Focus a large bill of materials.** If line-by-line review is impractical,
  identify high-value or high-risk items for direct analysis and consider a
  documented sample of the remaining items. State the selection method,
  value covered, results, and limits before drawing any conclusion about
  unreviewed lines. Do not project a sample result to all materials without
  an appropriate sampling basis; follow any applicable company or contract
  requirement for the scope of review.
- **Separate evidence types.** Put submitted supplier quotes in a quote table;
  put prior prices paid, dated manufacturer or catalog listings, marketplace
  prices, redacted invoices, independent estimates, and comparable-item
  research in a separate comparison table or paragraph. Record source, date,
  quantity, terms, and any adjustment. A single-unit public listing is a
  unit-price indicator, not a submitted offer or proven price for a larger
  quantity. Search the exact identifiable item first, then supported
  comparables, before telling the buyer public pricing is unavailable.
- **Evaluate ODC basis.** For proposed travel, use supplied trips, travelers,
  locations, duration, rates, and any stated reimbursement or ceiling terms;
  for equipment, rentals, software, supplies, and other expenses, use the
  relevant units, duration, quotes, or price history. Distinguish a fixed
  amount from an estimate, allowance, ceiling, or cost-reimbursable item.
  Identify unsupported quantities or unusually high rates and seek technical
  input when need or comparability is unclear. Do not assume travel is at
  cost, that only G&A applies, or that a published per-diem or fare is binding
  without the applicable contract terms.
- **Check lower-tier amounts.** When a proposed ODC is a lower-tier
  subcontract, identify its actual quote, selection basis, and supporting
  cost or price analysis where applicable. If a markup is separately proposed,
  show its stated base and amount and check for duplicate charges; do not
  infer an undisclosed markup or conclude it is reasonable from arithmetic
  alone.
- **Explain the result.** Show dollar and percentage variance against a valid
  comparison, including a delivery or lead-time premium when it affected
  best value. Call a list-price difference a comparison or documented
  discount, not negotiated savings unless a negotiation actually occurred.
  Make an element-level fair-and-reasonable finding only when the evidence
  supports that element. When a valid overall price comparison suffices and
  no separate element finding is required, state that a discussed component
  was included in the overall price rather than calling it Pending. Reserve
  Pending for a gap that prevents a required element or overall finding, and
  retain any supported overall-price finding.

Useful formulas, where inputs are available:

- Extended amount = quantity × unit price or hours × rate.
- Fee percentage = disclosed fee ÷ disclosed cost base × 100.
- Change percentage = (proposed − comparator) ÷ comparator × 100.
- Rate escalation percentage = (new rate ÷ earlier rate − 1) × 100.
- Monthly equivalent = period amount ÷ months in that period.

Round calculated percentage comparisons to two decimal places. Use precision
appropriate to the other data; avoid false precision. Explain
material differences and unresolved math before making a final finding.

Apply the contract-type lens: for **CPFF**, reconcile estimated cost, fixed
fee, fee base, and total; for **T&M/LH**, assess burdened rates, ceilings, and
separately proposed materials or other costs; for **FPLOE**, verify hours ×
rate against fixed period prices when those inputs exist; for **FFP**, assess
total price, line items, quantities, deliverables, and periods. For options
and modifications, explain material changes from the relevant baseline. For
new awards, use current offers, estimates, market data, or other valid support
even if no prior subcontract exists.
For a substantial modification with added and deleted work, distinguish the
price of added work from the credit for deleted work. Where cost-based detail
is supplied or required, distinguish deleted work already performed from
work avoided, and evaluate affected direct cost, indirect cost, and fee on
their supported bases. Reconcile those components to the net adjustment;
do not use the original estimate alone as proof of the current value of
deleted work or assume the net adjustment supports both sides of the change.
For cost-reimbursement structures, distinguish a reasonable proposed price or
ceiling from a realistic estimate of likely performance cost. If labor mix,
hours, material quantities, or ODC estimates create material uncertainty,
identify the technical or cost-realism review needed for the proposed action
or higher-tier price proposal; do not manufacture a probable cost from a
price comparison alone.
