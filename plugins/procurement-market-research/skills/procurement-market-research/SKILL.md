# Procurement Market Research

Research public suppliers, product or service options, pricing, contract
vehicles, and relevant procurement evidence. Deliver usable buyer evidence,
not a completed source selection or price-reasonableness determination.
Prioritize US-based suppliers when they can meet the requirement.

## Roles and directed messages

Unless the user defines the roles differently, **buyer** means the individual
purchasing professional; **Procurement** may also mean the department or its
review and approval function. The Program Manager, requester, and technical
owner describe the need and technical fit; they are not automatically the
buyer. Do not invent a “technical buyer” or transfer one role's decisions or
statements to another.

For a requested email or question list, identify the sender, recipient, and
purpose from the user's words and keep that direction throughout. From a
requester's perspective, “questions for my buyer” means internal questions to
the purchasing buyer, not questions for a vendor or technical evaluator. If
these roles are genuinely unclear and would change the content, ask one short
question; otherwise use a clearly labeled role placeholder. Draft the message
only; do not send it without authorization.

## Response sequence

1. **Scope:** Choose the requested deliverable. A request to conduct market
   research includes public supplier and vendor lookup; search and report
   useful findings up front. A request to identify requirements or risks
   **before** market research calls for a concrete intake assessment, without
   starting a supplier scan unless asked. Do not wait for a separate vendor
   details request when research is requested. Complete the requested work
   without adjacent deliverables, a work plan, or a routine “Would you like…”
   follow-up. Draft an RFQ, quote request, price analysis, negotiation
   record, or other downstream artifact only when the user asks for it. Do not
   contact a third party without authorization.
2. **Evidence:** Use supported facts and current sources as the specific route
   requires. Keep unknowns and material evidence gaps visible without turning
   them into a broad checklist of possible future work.
3. **Deliverable:** For a full draft or analysis, put the copy-ready output
   under **Draft / Results** first. Preserve required headings, findings,
   calculations, citations, and approval distinctions. After it, use
   **Procurement actions** (or **Property / Contracts actions**), **Why this
   matters**, **Sources**, and **Notes**, in that order, only for material
   information needed to interpret or use this output. Never mix directions or
   commentary into a copy-ready document or repeat its source list. For a
   focused question or edit, answer only that part without forcing headings.
4. **Validation:** Outside the copy-ready document, give one short reminder to
   validate the output against applicable company policies and procedures,
   contractual requirements, current regulations, and underlying evidence
   before use. Do not imply that every regulation applies to every purchase.
5. **Final trim:** Remove repeated explanations and speculative next steps.
   Use one plain sentence for a simple product or service description, short
   lists or tables for comparisons, and only the detail needed to support the
   conclusion. A routine renewal or straightforward request should normally
   fit on about one page, excluding essential source details; expand for the
   vendor options and evidence a market scan needs. Concision must not remove
   the actual supplier lookup. Define an unfamiliar acronym on first use and
   write for procurement, technical, and compliance readers.
6. **Quality check:** Confirm the purchase facts match the user's record,
   material claims have supplied or cited support, estimates and proposed
   targets are labeled, and any calculations work. Name only material facts
   still needing buyer verification. If the user corrects an assumption or
   narrows the task, revise the requested output directly instead of repeating
   process guidance.

## Route the request

Read the named files in `references/` before producing the corresponding
output. Follow only the branches the user needs. The original detailed
requirements are preserved in those files.

| Step | Route |
|---|---|
| 0. Pre-research readiness, when requested | Read `references/market-workflow.md`. Give the actual missing requirement questions, acquisition risks, and a short research-ready handoff from the supplied facts. Do not replace them with an explanation of how to do market research. Do not start a vendor scan unless the user also asks for one. |
| 1. Identify the buyer and requirement | For a requested market scan, read `references/market-workflow.md`. If a named item, provider, or service category is identifiable, begin public research even without quantity, budget, delivery location, or procurement context; label the comparison limits. Ask one grouped question only when the description is too vague to find credible sources. |
| 2. Research suppliers and options | For a market scan, open current OEM, supplier, reseller, and relevant contract-vehicle pages. Identify credible sellers and alternatives, compare the actual offering and public pricing or terms, and produce a substantive table with source links and dates. Do the lookup instead of telling the buyer to do it. |
| 3. Include useful vendor facts up front | Read the vendor and missing-data rules in `references/market-workflow.md`. Include each source's role, offering, public website or contact route, and verified channel or contract-vehicle facts when relevant. Add identifiers, size status, or award history when material and verifiable, or when requested; never invent CPARS access or ratings. |
| 4. Add classifications when relevant | Read `references/classifications.md` when a NAICS, PSC, or UNSPSC code is requested, a code is supplied for validation, or the acquisition strategy makes a code material. Do not add classification tables merely because an item was described. Verify codes, titles, and size standards in current authoritative sources. |
| 5. Apply regulatory and evidence limits | Read `references/regulatory-and-evidence.md` for every market research answer. When citing FAR/DFARS or advising a prime-contractor buyer, use the current official rule and actual contract context. Keep unsupported facts unknown. |

## Conclusions and boundaries

- Distinguish a submitted quote from public list, GSA, and historical award
  prices. Record source, date, configuration, quantity, and material terms.
- A missing search result is not proof that a supplier, contract vehicle,
  registration, or capability does not exist.
- Flag BAA/TAA and set-aside considerations only when relevant; verify the
  actual clause and buyer context before calling them requirements.
- Do not impose agency acquisition procedures on a prime contractor's own
  purchase without an applicable contract, clause, customer, or company basis.
- Do not invent prices, identifiers, certifications, past performance, codes,
  or technical equivalence. Identify the exact unresolved buyer-specific fact
  instead of assuming a default.
